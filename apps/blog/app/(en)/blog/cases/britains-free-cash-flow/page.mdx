A company can report a profit every year and still run out of money.

Profit is what the accounts say was earned. It rests on judgements: when a
sale counts, how fast a machine wears out, what a debt is still worth. Cash is
what actually arrived, and it is counted. The two drift apart all the time,
and most of the interesting questions about a business live in the gap.

So we asked one plain question of every company listed in the UK that has a
track record of digital annual reports: **does the business turn its profit
into cash, and what does it do with that cash?** This post explains the
measure, where the figures come from, how the workspace that reads them is
built, and what 385 companies turned out to do.

And what does it do with it?"](/blog/assets/images/uk-free-cash-flow-profit-reported-cash-counted.webp)

## What free cash flow is

The measure for this question is **free cash flow**.

- **Operating cash flow** is the cash the business brought in from what it
  does, after paying staff, suppliers and tax. It is a line of every
  cash-flow statement.
- **Capital expenditure** (capex) is what it paid for machines, buildings and
  software: in IFRS terms, purchases of property, plant and equipment and of
  intangible assets.
- **Free cash flow** is what is left: operating cash flow minus capex.

That is the cash a business leaves after keeping and growing its assets.
Lenders, dividends and share buybacks are paid out of it. When it is negative
year after year, the money comes from somewhere else: savings, new shares or
new debt.

A real year. NEXT plc, the retailer, in the year to 31 January 2026, from its
own tagged filing:

| £ million | |
| --- | ---: |
| Operating cash flow | 1,233.6 |
| − Purchases of property, plant and equipment | 135.4 |
| − Purchases of intangible assets | 35.8 |
| **= Free cash flow** | **1,062.4** |
| Profit for the year | 898.0 |
| Free cash flow as a share of profit | **118 %** |
| Dividends paid | 286.5 |
| Share buybacks | 131.4 |

Every pound of profit arrived as cash, and more: depreciation is a cost in the
income statement but not a payment. About £420 million went back to
shareholders.

![NEXT plc's year to January 2026 on a typed sheet: operating cash flow 1,233.6, capital expenditure 171.2, free cash flow 1,062.4 marked in lime, profit 898.0, free cash flow ÷ profit 118 %; beside it a cash box and tabs for dividends 286.5, buybacks 131.4 and about 420 back to shareholders](/blog/assets/images/next-plc-free-cash-flow-worked-example.webp)

One thing to keep in mind from the start: **free cash flow is not a line in
IFRS accounts.** Companies that report it define it themselves, as an
*alternative performance measure*. This workspace uses the plain formula above
for every company, so that they can be compared, and keeps what companies call
free cash flow as a question of its own (below).

## Where the figures come from

For financial years starting on or after 1 January 2021, issuers with
securities on a UK regulated market publish their annual financial report in
the European Single Electronic Format (FCA rule DTR 4.1.14). The report is a
web page (XHTML) in which every figure of the primary statements carries a
tag from the IFRS taxonomy: operating cash flow is
`ifrs-full:CashFlowsFromUsedInOperatingActivities` in every report, whoever
designed the page.

![A drawn annual-report page with three figures outlined in lime and their IFRS element names under them, next to a print of an old records archive and a print of a punch-card store](/blog/assets/images/esef-tagged-annual-report-ifrs-elements.webp)

**XBRL International**, the not-for-profit body behind the XBRL standard,
collects these reports at [filings.xbrl.org](https://filings.xbrl.org/). For
each filing it offers an Inline XBRL viewer, every tagged figure as xBRL-JSON,
the report package as filed, and the validator's messages. In its own words
there are, at present, "no restrictions on the ways that the data can be
used", and "the repository is not complete". Filings arrive from the national
collection mechanisms, sometimes well after the original submission.

![The filings.xbrl.org index filtered to country GB, in a browser](/blog/assets/images/filings-xbrl-org-gb-filings-index.webp)

That is why we read it:

- **The figures are the company's own**, tagged by the company, not guessed
  from a PDF by a scraper.
- **The tags line up** across companies and across years, so years can be laid
  side by side without a mapping table per company.
- **Anyone can check them** against the same page we read, figure by figure.
- **It needs no key and no account.** The JSON:API at
  `filings.xbrl.org/api/filings` lists 500 filings a request.

## Who is in

A company is in when it has **at least three reporting periods on the index**
and **a filing submitted this year**: a track record, and still current. Then
every one of its filings is read, however old, back to 2020 for some.

One surprise on the way: **"GB" on the index means filed with the UK
regulator, not British.** The FCA's list includes every issuer with
securities in London: Kazakh, Russian, Korean, Japanese, Hong Kong and
Bermudan groups, the Irish groups, and a British retailer incorporated in
Luxembourg. Of the 444 companies that met the rule, **59 name a domicile or
country of incorporation outside the UK** in their own filings, and they are
left out. A first, naive filter also dropped about sixty British companies,
because reports converted from PDF print the place as "En gl an d & Wal es";
spaces are now ignored when the place is read.

What remains: **385 British companies, 1,823 filings** with periods ending
2020 to 2026, and **12 printed annual reports** (2,890 pages) of the largest
British non-financial groups, read page by page.

![A rule card (three or more reporting periods, a filing this year), a stack of binders, and a print of Tower Bridge in fog captioned 385 British companies; beside it 385 British companies, 1,823 filings, 12 printed annual reports](/blog/assets/images/uk-cash-flow-selection-385-british-companies.webp)

## The workspace: three sources and their lineage

In Classifyre this is one workspace with three sources, each a small Python
notebook:

| Source | One record per | What it reads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **UK ESEF filings** | filing | the index, newest first; each filing's xBRL-JSON (cash-flow statement, income statement, balance sheet, current and comparative year) and its validation messages |
| **UK company profiles** | company, and one benchmark per financial year | computed from the filings: a company's years side by side, converted to pounds at ECB cross rates; a benchmark holds the medians of every company in a year |
| **UK annual reports** | printed report | the twelve largest British non-financial groups by revenue, cut into pages so that a finding can point at one |

The first source checks the index on a schedule, so a company's next report
joins by itself, and a company that files its first report of a new year
joins the workspace.

The connectors also **declare lineage**: a profile names the filings it was
computed from, a benchmark names the profiles behind its medians, a filing
names the printed report it was read from. Open NEXT's profile and its five
annual filings and every benchmark it went into are one click away.

![The lineage graph of NEXT plc's company profile: its annual filings feeding it, and the yearly benchmarks it feeds, in the Classifyre asset page](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-lineage-company-profile-filings-benchmarks.webp)

## The vocabulary: glossary and entities

Records hold figures. A question needs words, and the words live in the
glossary: **84 concepts in six groups** (free cash flow, IFRS figures, data
quality, industries, multi-year trends, kinds of record), each defined in one
sentence. A *cash machine*, for example, is "positive free cash flow two years
running, at least 10% of revenue, and at least 80% of profit: the profit
arrives as cash."

Every kind of finding a detector can produce is **bound** to one of these
concepts. The detector says `cash_machine`; the glossary knows what that means
and that it sits under *strong free cash flow*. That binding is what lets a
standing question be asked in words rather than in detector keys.

![The glossary concept Cash machine in Classifyre: 81 assets, 81 findings, its binding to the detector output cash_machine](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-glossary-cash-machine-binding.webp)

The companies are in the same glossary, as **entities**: 385 of them, declared
by the profiles source. A small pattern detector finds each company's Legal
Entity Identifier (LEI) in every record, so a company's filings, its profile
and its printed report all land on one entity page.

## The readers: rules, a decision model, a language model

Ten detectors read the records. **Most are plain Python rules** that anyone can
read: does the cash-flow statement add up, what is the free cash flow, what do
three to six years show together, where does a company stand in its
industry. Subtracting two numbers needs no model.

Two questions do, and only there does the workspace use one.

### A decision model for the industry

A free cash flow margin means little without peers: a software company and a
water company should not be held to the same number. **No ESEF tag says what
industry a company is in.** What every company does give, in its filing, is a
description of its principal activities in its own words.

So a **decision model** reads that text and picks one of eleven industries,
and also answers whether the company is a financial institution. A decision
model is not a chatbot: it gets the question and the options, each with a
description, and returns a probability for every option, no text. Here it is
Perplexity's Decider (`pplx-decider-v1.2`) through OpenRouter. (We wrote about
[what decision models are](/blog/articles/decision-models-system-one-explained/)
separately.)

For Rio Tinto, whose filing says its business is "finding, mining, and
processing mineral resources", the answer was *materials* at 99.6%. A Python
rule then places each company's free cash flow margin within its industry:
top quarter, bottom quarter, or capex far above the industry's.

![On a table: a print of a sorting office with pigeonholes, Rio Tinto's own description of its business with the words finding, mining and processing mineral resources marked, and the probability the decision model gave each of eleven industries, materials 99.6](/blog/assets/images/decision-model-industry-probabilities-rio-tinto.webp)

### A language model for what companies call free cash flow

What a company means by free cash flow lives in the **text** of its report, not
in its tags, and the printed reports run to as many as 396 pages (BP's). A
**language model**, GLM 5.3 Flash, reads only the pages that use the term:
a pattern on the detector (`content_pattern`) decides which, in eighteen
languages. That came to about **one page in twenty** (142 of 2,890).

On each page it writes down the figure, the page, the words used and the
definition, and whether leases, interest and acquisitions are deducted.
Unilever, page 46: "cash flow from operating activities, less income taxes
paid, net capital expenditure and net interest payments." A Python rule then
sets the stated figure against operating cash flow minus capex from the same
report's tags.

![A thick report beside a grid of a hundred and twenty small pages, six of them outlined in lime and the rest dimmed, a magnifying glass over them, tags reading GLM 5.3 Flash and "only pages that use the term, about 1 in 20"](/blog/assets/images/llm-reads-free-cash-flow-pages-one-in-twenty.webp)

## Questions that stay asked

Findings are answers. An investigation needs **questions that stay asked**:
the workspace holds thirty of them as *inquiry watches*, each asked through
glossary concepts. *Which companies are cash machines? Who leads its industry
on free cash flow? Whose cash-flow statement does not add up?*

**Eight cases** are built on them, each one question, each with a desk:

| Case | Asks |
| --- | --- |
| Cash machines | Who turns profit into cash, year after year? |
| Compounders | Who grew capital and cash in every year? |
| Paying out what they did not earn | Who returned more than it generated, and borrowed the difference? |
| Growth that costs cash | Whose growth eats cash; who is in an investment cycle? |
| Profit without cash | Whose earnings never arrive as money? |
| Numbers that don't add up | How reliable are UK digital annual reports? |
| What companies call free cash flow | How far is a company's own figure from the formula? |
| Can search find the number? | Do tagged reports make a ground truth for search? |

A case **pulls new answers in** as scans land them, and lets a company go when
its next report no longer supports the finding. Each case also states two or
three **hypotheses**, claims that sound right, written down before they were
tested. The workspace counts each one on the data, writes the tally into a
dated note on the hypothesis, links a few findings for and against, and marks
it. Of 23 hypotheses, 12 came out supported, **9 refuted** and 2
inconclusive. The refuted ones are the most interesting.

![The case board of Cash machines in Classifyre: the hypothesis "Cash machines keep most of their cash" stamped SUPPORTED, lines to six companies' cash_machine findings, and its dated note: 69 cash machines paid dividends; the median paid out 35% of its free cash flow](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-case-board-cash-machines-hypothesis.webp)

## What 385 companies did with their cash

All figures are the hypotheses' tallies of 21 October 2026, on non-financial
companies.

**Half are steady.** Of 231 companies with four or more years, **117 (51%)
never had a year of negative free cash flow**. *(Most UK companies generate
cash every year: supported.)*

**Profit compounds more often than cash, but not by much.** Of 232 companies
with three or more years, 61 grew profit at least 10% a year; **44 grew free
cash flow that fast with every year positive.** The cliché says "far more
often". *(Refuted.)*

**Real compounders are rare, and pay their own way.** Twelve companies grew
their equity by 10% or more in every year, **Greggs, NEXT and Halma among
them; eleven of the twelve did it without borrowing** or paying out more than
they generated. *(Compounders grow on their own cash, not on debt:
supported.)*

![A typed card: equity up 10% or more in every year, 12 companies, 11 self-funded, among them Greggs, NEXT and Halma, beside a print of a drawing office; the hypothesis "Compounders grow on their own cash, not on debt" stamped SUPPORTED](/blog/assets/images/uk-capital-compounders-greggs-next-halma.webp)

**Cash machines keep most of what they make.** Of 69 cash machines that paid
dividends, the median paid out **35% of its free cash flow**. *(Supported.)*

**Paying out more than the business generates, while debt rises, is the
exception.** Of 169 companies that paid dividends in three or more years,
**18 (11%)** paid out more in dividends and buybacks than they generated over
the period while net debt rose. *(One UK dividend payer in ten borrows to pay
out: supported.)* And the usual suspect is wrong: where payouts exceed cash,
**dividends, not buybacks, are the larger part for 18 of 21.** *(Buybacks,
not dividends, open the gap: refuted.)* In a single year it is more common:
32 of 213 dividend payers paid more than that year's free cash flow (15%).

![A typed card: 18 of 169 paid out more than they generated while net debt rose, beside a print of pound coins; two hypotheses, "One UK dividend payer in ten borrows to pay out" stamped SUPPORTED and "Buybacks, not dividends, open the gap" stamped REFUTED](/blog/assets/images/uk-dividends-beyond-free-cash-flow-18-of-169.webp)

**Fast growth that burns cash is not an investment phase.** Eight companies
grew revenue 10% or more a year while burning cash over the period; **none of
them had raised its capex share**, and five of the eight raised their net debt
by half or more. *(Investment-led: refuted. Paid for with debt: supported.)*

**IFRS 16 hides less than people think.** Since 2019 most rent is financing
cash flow, outside the usual formula. Of 191 companies with positive free cash
flow that tag lease payments, only **8 (4%)** turn negative once those
payments are deducted. *(IFRS 16 hides cash burn: refuted.)*

**Where interest is shown matters.** IAS 7 lets a company show interest paid
under operating or under financing cash flow. 102 of 385 show it under
financing; for 50 it is worth at least a tenth of free cash flow, and **8 cash
machines would fall below the 10% margin** if they showed it the way their
peers do.

**Asset-light industries keep more of their revenue.** The median free cash
flow margin of technology, health care and consumer staples companies is
**11.5%**, against **7.7%** for utilities, energy, materials and telecoms.
*(Supported.)*

The largest free cash flow in the latest year: Shell (£16.65bn), BP
(£8.55bn), AstraZeneca (£6.58bn), Vodafone (£6.03bn), Unilever (£5.79bn).

## What companies call free cash flow

Ten of the twelve printed reports state a free cash flow of their own. Two
things stand out.

- **It is usually stricter than the formula.** For 8 of the 10, the company's
  own figure is lower than operating cash flow minus capex on the same
  report's tags: UK companies also deduct interest, tax, leases or
  investments. For BP and Rio Tinto it is higher. *(Companies' own free cash
  flow is usually higher than the formula: refuted.)*
- **It comes with an adjective.** Six of the ten qualify the name: BP's
  *adjusted* and *modified* free cash flow, Compass's *underlying*,
  Sainsbury's *retail*, M&S's *free cash flow from operations*, Vodafone's
  *adjusted*. The name already tells you it is their own measure.

So two companies' "free cash flow" are rarely the same number, which is the
reason this workspace computes one formula for everyone and keeps the
companies' own figures beside it, with their definitions.

## How reliable are the digital reports?

The tags are the company's own, which cuts both ways.

- **Four in five annual reports** of non-financial companies tag operating
  cash flow and capex with standard IFRS elements (1,219 of 1,520); for 57
  more, free cash flow can be read only through the company's own capex
  element.
- **7% of filings** (131 of 1,823) have tagged figures that contradict each
  other: a cash-flow statement that does not add up, one figure with two
  values, statements that disagree, a payment with its sign reversed, a filing
  indexed for one year that holds another. The share is flat, not falling:
  4%, 9%, 8%, 6%, 8% for 2021 to 2025.
- **Tagging capex with a company's own element is a habit**, not a teething
  problem: 13% of filings in 2021, 16 to 17% every year since 2023. A screen
  that reads only standard tags misses that investment and overstates free
  cash flow. Diageo tags capex as
  `PurchaseOfPropertyPlantAndEquipmentAndComputerSoftware`; RELX, Unilever,
  Diageo and Tesco tag their buybacks with elements of their own. Without
  reading those, the payouts case would miss most of the FTSE's buybacks.

## Can search find the number?

Every tagged figure is a verified answer printed on a known page, which makes
the twelve printed reports a free test of document search. We asked the
workspace's search 201 questions written from the tags
(`text-embedding-3-small`, 2,890 pages):

| Question | Right report first | Passage on the page that prints the figure |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| In plain words ("BP's operating cash flow for 2025") | 97–100% | 23% |
| In the statement's own words | 91–100% | 29% |
| The figure alone ("which page shows 24,483?"), meaning only | 10% | 1.6% |
| The figure alone, hybrid search (meaning and words) | 24% | **48%** |

Search by meaning finds the right report nearly every time (the company's name
does the work) but prefers the note that *discusses* a figure to the
statement that *prints* it. And embeddings do not read digits: a figure alone
found its page 1.6% of the time until hybrid search learned to match the
words of passages, figures included.

## What it does not tell you

- **Free cash flow here is the textbook formula.** A company's own measure may
  suit that company better.
- **Banks, insurers, investment trusts, property companies and parent-only
  accounts are not judged**, by their tags, their investment property share or
  the industry the model reads.
- **The industry is a model's reading,** and a model can be wrong.
- **Capex from a company's own element is matched by name**; the anchoring
  ESEF requires is not in the xBRL-JSON.
- **"Three years" means three reporting periods on the index**, where UK
  filings go back only to 2020/21.
- **A group and its filing subsidiary can both be in** (Rolls-Royce Holdings
  and Rolls-Royce plc, BT Group and British Telecommunications). The medians
  barely move.
- **Amounts are in the company's currency**, with pounds at ECB rates beside
  them.

And above all: **a finding is a question to ask about a company, not a
verdict on it.**
