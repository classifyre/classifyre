Friday, ten o'clock, Munich. Nine minutes into the planning meeting somebody
says it: *the housings have moved to the eighteenth.*

Five hundred pump housings from Elbe Precision Castings, due on 4 October,
will arrive two weeks late. Three customer orders need them between the 5th
and the 7th. Together they are worth **€870,000**.

Everyone in the room has an idea. *Hamburg has stock.* *Somebody found a cheap
lot of a thousand.* *We booked a second supplier for exactly this.*

> **The short answer:** none of the three holds. Each fails for a reason that
> sits on a different desk: a truck slot, an engineering revision, a foundry
> that two suppliers share. A fourth idea arrives by memo at half past eleven.
> It covers every order, and it is still only an offer.

## The same story as a film

*One bad Friday* is this case told in five minutes.

![Title card of the film One bad Friday: a planning-meeting table, a note moving a delivery from 4 to 18 October, three customer orders and the title set over them](/blog/assets/images/one-bad-friday-supply-chain-case-film.webp)

The pictures in this article are stills from it. Paper on a black table is the
film explaining; a dark browser window is the workspace itself. The
photographs were generated for the film and show no real place or product.

## The answer was already in the building

On a Friday like this nobody lacks data. The orders are in the ERP. The stock
is in the warehouse system. The truck slots are with the carrier. And the
reason the delivery moved is in a memo somebody wrote at 07:15.

What is missing is one place where those four meet. So the workspace connects
**seven desks**:

| Source | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Company master data** | Suppliers, foundries, customers, plants, machines, parts, pumps |
| **Supply & orders** | Orders, the purchase order and its new date, stock, truck slots, offers, the operations note |
| **Purchasing & costing** | Steel quotes, resin offers, the finishing-station sheet, sourcing notes |
| **Machines & maintenance** | Ninety days of hourly readings from 16 machines, maintenance history, handovers |
| **Quality & traceability** | 400 housing inspections, 2,000 final inspections, shipments, finished lots |
| **Inventory control** | Stock, lots, reservations and transfers in three plants |
| **Data integration feeds** | SAP, MES, warehouse, order-event and quality-device extracts, and the tickets that explain them |

Together that is **196 records**. Eight detectors read them. Six are *checks*,
one per desk: plain rules that say what is wrong in one sentence and show the
arithmetic. This is one, word for word:

> PO101 from Elbe Precision Castings (500 housings) now arrives 18 Oct, 14 days
> after the promised 4 Oct — too late for all 3 orders that need it.

The seventh detector finds the sentences in memos that change what is allowed.
The eighth finds names and codes, so that a supplier is the same supplier
wherever it is written. Both come back later.

## Four ideas, each with a test

Every rescue idea goes on the case board as a **hypothesis**, with the test it
has to pass. For the first one: *if true, the stock that can reach Munich by
4 October is at least 380 housings.*

### 1. "Hamburg has stock"

It does. Hamburg shows 260 housings. But 160 of them protect reservations and
safety stock, which leaves 100 free. And the only truck slot that arrives in
time carries 80.

That saves one order. **€570,000** stays exposed. The check says it in its own
words: *"Hamburg has 100 free housings, but the only truck slot arriving by
4 Oct carries 80: 20 housings that exist cannot reach Munich in time."*
**Refuted.**

### 2. "There is a cheap lot of a thousand"

A fourth order, for sanitary pumps, is short as well, and Adria Value Metals
offers 1,000 housings at €145 apiece. They are revision C with industrial
approval. That pump takes revision D with sanitary approval.

![An offer for 1,000 housings at 145 euros, revision C, next to a memo with one sentence marked: purchasing cannot waive this requirement, and the two findings that quote it](/blog/assets/images/cheap-housing-lot-wrong-revision-memo-sentence.webp)

Whether that can be waived is not in any table. It is in the operations note,
and the memo detector found the sentence and quoted it next to the price:
*"Revision C has a different seal interface; purchasing cannot waive this
requirement."* **Refuted.**

### 3. "We are dual-sourced"

This is the comfortable one. The next hundred housings are booked with two
suppliers, Elbe and Morava, and the booking sheet has two ticks on it.

![Two supplier cards, Elbe and Morava, tied by lime thread to one photograph of a foundry stamped down from 2 to 16 October, with the finding shared upstream dependency under them](/blog/assets/images/two-suppliers-one-foundry-shared-dependency.webp)

One level up, both have their housings cast at the same foundry, and that
foundry is down from 2 to 16 October. All 100 booked housings fall inside the
outage. Two names, one foundry. **Refuted**, and the most dangerous of the
three, because the purchasing sheet looked fine.

### 4. The memo at half past eleven

At 11:30 a procurement note lands. A fourth supplier offers 200 finished
housings, cast at a different foundry and released by engineering for exactly
these pumps.

![A procurement note with a recovery offer, and under it a bar: 100 housings in Munich, 80 from Hamburg, 200 from the new lot, 380 of 380, with all three orders marked covered](/blog/assets/images/recovery-offer-380-of-380-housings-covered.webp)

100 in Munich, 80 from Hamburg, 200 from the new lot: 380 of 380. Every order
is covered. The board moves this hypothesis to **supported, at 75%**, and says
why it is not more: it is an offer, not stock, it expires that afternoon, and
the same memo states that no purchase or transfer has been approved.

![The case board in Classifyre, zoomed out: three hypothesis frames marked refuted and a fourth marked supported at 75 percent, with the ten o'clock supply position and the noon update both on the board](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-case-board-three-refuted-one-supported.webp)

Ten o'clock is still there. The position as it was known then and the
position at noon are two records on the same board. Newer evidence changed the
verdict, not the history. (The labels at the sides of the board are the
film's notes; everything inside the window is the workspace.)

## One supplier, four desks

Now the part no single system shows. Take one name from that Friday: Elbe
Precision Castings. It is late on the purchase order. It shares a foundry
with its rival. It cast the housing batch behind a quality complaint. And it
is the incumbent in a steel quote.

![The entity page of Elbe Precision Castings in Classifyre: 21 mentions in 13 records from four sources, its supplier code and its name as identifiers, and the list of every record that mentions it](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-entity-page-one-supplier-four-sources.webp)

Four desks, thirteen records, one page. A memo says "S001", a sheet says
"Elbe Precision Castings", and both land on the same entity, because to the
workspace a supplier is a thing, not a string.

## The machine that was not the leak

That quality complaint: pumps are leaking in the field. There is an obvious
suspect, machine M001. Its vibration has gone from 2.0 to 8.0 mm/s in a week,
inside its own operating mode. That part is true, and an inspection the next
day finds the bearing damaged.

![A table of failed housing inspections, batch by machine: the clean batch fails 2 and 3 percent, the suspect batch 20 and 18 percent, on a drifting machine and on a steady one](/blog/assets/images/defects-follow-the-batch-not-the-machine.webp)

But the inspections say something else. A clean batch on that machine fails
2%. The suspect batch fails 18% on a machine that is perfectly steady. The
defects follow the material. The bearing needed replacing; it just was not
the leak.

M001 is the same record in both cases: the failing machine in the maintenance
case, and the alibi in the quality one. Without that link the bearing would
have been blamed for leaks it did not cause. With it, the claim stays what it
is: an association strong enough to act on, not a proven cause. No lab report
exists yet, and the case says so.

## Three more numbers from the same Friday

![Three sheets: a steel quote 9 percent off that costs 4,188 euros more, a machine at 190 degrees that is 87.8 degrees Celsius, and a shortage of 380 gasket sets that is a stock of 420](/blog/assets/images/steel-discount-fahrenheit-alarm-gasket-boxes.webp)

- **Steel at 9% off** costs **€4,188 more** per 10,000 usable kilograms. The
  price is per kilogram bought; at the cheaper supplier's 86% yield you buy a
  lot more of them.
- **A machine at 190 degrees.** Fahrenheit: the sensor had been switched on
  30 September and the label had not. It is 87.8 °C, under a 95 °C alarm.
- **380 gasket sets short.** SAP's count was a day old. The warehouse had 35
  boxes of twelve: 420, against 400 needed. Nothing to buy.

Each is a finding in a different case, from a different check. The workspace
holds more of the same kind: a resin deal that would expire in storage, a
scrap "crisis" that was a change of product mix, a retried approval that did
not reserve anything twice.

## The count

Six cases. **Twenty-five hypotheses. Twenty refuted.**

And twelve standing questions stay on watch. One of them asks: *where do two
suppliers rely on the same source?* It is asked by meaning, through the
glossary concept "hidden shared dependency", not by keyword, so the next scan
that finds one puts it in front of you. Before the Friday.

## What it does not do

It does not decide. And it will not let a figure pass for more than it is:

- Revenue at risk is exposure, not a loss.
- An offer is not stock.
- Exposed units are not defective units.
- A short window after a repair does not prove reliability.

Those caveats are written into the findings and quoted from the memos: *"No
purchase or transfer has been approved yet."* Somebody still has to make the
call that afternoon.
