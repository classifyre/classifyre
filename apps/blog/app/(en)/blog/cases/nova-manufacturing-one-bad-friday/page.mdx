It is Friday, ten o'clock, in Munich. The planning meeting is nine minutes old when someone says the sentence
everyone dreads: *the housings have moved to the 18th.*

Five hundred pump housings from Elbe Precision Castings, due on 4 October, will now arrive two weeks late. Three customer
orders need them between the 5th and the 7th. Together they are worth **€870,000**. Customer service would like a list of
people to call before lunch.

Everyone in the room has an idea.

*Hamburg has stock.* Somebody found a cheap lot of a thousand. *We booked a second supplier for exactly this.*
And at half past eleven a recovery offer arrives with a 15:00 deadline.

Which of those ideas survive contact with the data?

> **The short answer:** one of the four does, and only conditionally. The other three each fail for a reason that
> sits in a different system: a truck slot, an engineering revision, a foundry that two "different" suppliers
> share. This is the story of the namespace we built to find that out in minutes, and of five more questions like it
> at the same factory.

Nova Manufacturing is fictional. Every number is invented, and none of it belongs to a real company. But each trap in
this article is one that real manufacturers will recognise, because the namespace was built from the problems they bring us.

## The company

Nova makes industrial pumps in three plants: **Munich**, **Hamburg** and **Brno**. It sells three models (a utility
pump, a duplex industrial pump and a sanitary process pump) to four customers, runs twenty machines, sixteen of them
with sensors, and buys from four tier-one suppliers, some of which depend in turn on two foundries Nova never deals with directly.

That is a small company on purpose. The point is not scale. It is that the *same few names* turn up in very different
places: a supplier in the purchasing sheet, in a quality report, in a maintenance note and in a data ticket. Nobody has
joined them up. That is the real Friday-morning problem.

## What is in the namespace

Seven sources, each a business desk rather than a technology:

| Source | What it holds | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| **Company master data** | Suppliers, foundries, customers, plants, machines, parts, pumps | The directory everything else points at |
| **Supply & orders** | Orders, purchase orders, warehouse stock, truck slots, supplier offers, the supplier-operations note | What planners see when a delivery slips |
| **Purchasing & costing** | Steel quotes, resin offers, the finishing-station sheet, sourcing notes | Where "cheaper" gets tested |
| **Machines & maintenance** | 34,560 hourly readings from 16 machines, maintenance history, M003's temperature feed, handovers | Which machine is really sick |
| **Quality & traceability** | 400 housing inspections, 2,000 final inspections, shipments, finished lots, the quality log | Does the defect follow the batch or the machine |
| **Inventory control** | Stock, lots, reservations and transfers in three plants | Why stock on paper is not stock you can use |
| **Data integration feeds** | SAP, MES, warehouse, order-event and quality-device extracts, plus stewardship tickets | Where the data itself can lie |

Together they contain 196 business records, twelve of them memos. On top of them sit:

- **Eight detectors** that read those records and say what is wrong, in plain sentences, showing their arithmetic.
- **A glossary of 65 concepts** (what a "late delivery", a "hidden shared dependency" or a "unit mismatch" *is*),
  plus **44 named entities** such as Elbe Precision Castings or Machine M001.
- **Twelve standing questions** that keep watching for things like "where do two suppliers rely on the same source?"
- **Six cases**, one per question below, each testing competing explanations as hypotheses.

## Six questions, six obvious answers that were wrong

### 1. The delayed delivery: who gets hurt, and what can we do?

Four rescue ideas, laid side by side as hypotheses.

**"Hamburg's stock covers it."** Hamburg shows 260 released housings, so it looks easy. But 160 protect local
reservations and safety stock, 100 are free, and the only truck slot that arrives in time carries **80**. That saves
one order (€300,000) and leaves €570,000 uncovered. *Refuted.*

**"The cheap thousand will do."** For the sanitary pump, Adria Value Metals offers 1,000 housings at €145. They are
revision C with industrial approval; the pump needs revision D with sanitary approval, and engineering cannot waive
it. The only compliant offer that arrives on time covers 60 of the 80 needed. *Refuted.*

**"We are dual-sourced."** Elbe and Morava are two names on the booking sheet. Look one level up and both cast at
**Central Alloy Works**, which is in a confirmed outage from 2 to 16 October. All 100 booked housings fall inside it.
*Refuted, and the most dangerous of the four.*

**"The recovery lot covers everything."** Nord Independent Foundry has 200 finished housings from a different foundry,
released by engineering for exactly these pumps, arriving on the 5th. With it, 380 housings meet 380 of demand. This
one is *supported*, at 75% confidence, because it is still only an offer and it expires at 15:00 today.

### 2. Is the cheaper option really cheaper?

Three purchasing decisions where the lowest number is not the answer.

- **Steel.** Adria quotes 9% less per kilogram than Elbe. But prices are per kilogram *bought* and demand is per
  kilogram *usable*. At Adria's 86% yield, 10,000 usable kilograms cost **€53,372** against **€49,184**: €4,188 *more*.
- **Resin.** A quarter-end offer at €4.00 per kilogram, minimum 5,000 kg. Storage holds 4,000 and already contains 600.
  1,600 kg has nowhere to go, and 3,000 kg would still be sitting there when everything expires on 30 October.
  A smaller offer at €5.20 fits, wastes nothing and commits €10,400 instead of €20,000.
- **Pumps.** The duplex pump earns more *per pump*. The utility pump earns more *per finishing hour*, and finishing
  hours are the bottleneck. With a binding contract minimum, the best feasible plan is 80 + 20 for €80,000.

Then a twist. On 5 October engineering pilots a revised cutting process and Adria's yield climbs to 97%. The
discount *survives*: €1,864 cheaper, but only as a modelled saving from three pilot runs, for the next order only.
The case keeps both conclusions, with dates, because both were right when they were written.

### 3. Which machine should maintenance visit first?

Sixteen machines send readings every hour. A plant-wide alert line makes **M002** look sick. It has run at 6.0 mm/s
for ninety days in its approved high-speed mode. It is simply loud.

**M001** looks fine against that line, yet inside its *own* mode it has gone from 2.0 to 8.0 mm/s and from 60 to
80 °C in a week. It has had two bearing repairs and €150,000 of orders assigned. Inspected on 3 October: outer-race
spalling. After the swap it reads 2.2 mm/s and 61 °C, while the untouched control M002 hasn't moved.

And **M003**, which was "running at 190 degrees"? Its sensor was switched to Fahrenheit on 30 September while the
adapter kept labelling it Celsius. 190 °F is 87.8 °C, the reading is a day old, and the independent reference sensor
says 87.8 °C against a 95 °C alarm. No overheating.

### 4. Why are pumps leaking: the batch, the machine, or the product mix?

Housing batch B994 from Elbe fails 19% of inspections. B990 from Morava fails 2.5%. The gap shows on *both*
machines. The defects follow the material.

This is where the namespace earns its keep. M001 is a fair suspect: it really is drifting. But the evidence is one click
away in a different source. Batch B990 running on the same M001 fails only 2%, and the perfectly steady M002 fails 18%
on B994. The failing bearing is real, and it is not what is leaking the pumps.

Meanwhile the scrap rate jumped from 1.9% to 9.1% and operations wants to stop the line. Within each product nothing
moved: one stayed at 1%, the other at 10%. What changed was the mix, from 10% to 90% of the harder pump. At the same
mix, both months score 5.5%.

And lot FG42, 30 units for Danube Bioprocess, had no material scan. Unknown is not clean. Containment was 50 to 80
units until a recovered production traveler showed 12 units on B994 and 18 on B990: **exactly 62 exposed**.

### 5. Plenty of stock on paper: why do we still run short?

Hamburg shows 1,300 seal cartridges while Munich needs 400. Only 650 are free (the rest is reserved, safety or
quarantined), and the trucks carry 250 from Hamburg and 180 from Brno. The cheapest feasible plan is 250 + 130 for
**€1,270**. The emergency purchase would cost €17,480 and arrive on 23 October.

Resin is worse: 700 kg on paper, 20 kg usable. One lot expires before it is needed, 180 kg of the other is reserved,
and the cheap route isn't cold-chain. 100 kg stay unresolved, and the case says so rather than inventing a fix.

Then a retry. An approval for Hamburg's bearing kits was repeated, and someone worried it reserved the stock twice. It
didn't: the system returned the original decision. Adding up every response would claim 280 kits; distinct reservations
are 160.

### 6. Which of our numbers can we trust?

Four alarms arrive on Friday morning. Three were manufactured by the data.

- *"380 gasket sets short."* The warehouse counts 35 boxes of 12, which is 420 pieces. SAP's 20 are 27 hours old.
  A look-alike part with 900 pieces isn't the same part. No purchase needed.
- *"340 impellers needed."* Five messages, one delivered twice, one old version replayed after a newer one. The real
  current demand is 60.
- *"Lot QT61 breached 95 degrees."* It reported 185 under a schema that means Fahrenheit. That is 85 °C.
- *"Lot QT63 reads 2,040."* This one really was unresolved until the producer recovered the missing contract: the field
  is conductivity, not temperature. All checks pass. Quality may *review* the lot. Nothing has been released.

## The threads that join the cases

The best moments weren't inside any one case.

**Elbe Precision Castings appears in four sources.** It is late on a purchase order, it shares a foundry with a supplier
it competes with, it sells the housings in batch B994, and its steel is the incumbent in a cost sheet. No single system
shows that. The entity page does.

**Machine M001 appears in two cases.** It is the deteriorating machine in one and the alibi in the other. Without the
cross-link, the failing bearing would have been blamed for leaks it didn't cause.

**One foundry, two apparent suppliers.** Central Alloy Works is the quiet villain in the supply case, and its outage
window is in the register, in the notes, and in a standing question that will catch the next one.

## What makes it more than a dashboard

A dashboard shows numbers. This namespace shows the **reasoning**.

- **Checks that show their work.** Every finding is a sentence a planner can read ("SO102 cannot be completed by
  6 Oct: 40 of 100 housings are still missing even after the best available transfer"). The numbers are computed from
  the records, not typed in, so change a quantity and the finding changes with it.
- **A shared vocabulary.** "Late delivery", "hidden shared dependency" and "unit mismatch" are defined once, in plain
  English. Every finding is bound to the concept it means, so a standing question about *supply risk* reaches findings
  from three different checks.
- **Named things, not strings.** Suppliers, customers, plants, machines and parts are entities. A memo that says
  "S001", a sheet that says "Elbe Precision Castings" and a batch record that names its supplier all land on the same page.
- **Competing explanations.** Each case keeps hypotheses with evidence *for* and *against*, and a log that explains
  every change of verdict. Newer evidence changes the verdict, not the history.
- **Quoted memos.** The sentences in the notes that change what is allowed ("purchasing cannot waive this
  requirement") are found and quoted verbatim, so a number can always be set next to the sentence behind it.

## What it deliberately does not claim

Honesty is built in. Revenue at risk is exposure, not a loss. A probability is a planning input, not a prediction.
Exposed units are not defective units. A short post-repair window doesn't prove reliability. A recovered paper trail
is not a release. An offer isn't stock. These caveats are in the findings themselves, because the first thing a good
analyst asks is *what can't we say?*
