A company is about to be sold. The buyer's lawyers open the data room and
find 200 contracts. They ask every one of them the same questions:

- Who signed it?
- When, and until when?
- How much notice does it take to end it?
- Which law governs it?

Each answer is written somewhere in the contract. *"This Agreement shall be
governed by the laws of the State of Delaware"* sits on page fourteen. Finding
it two hundred times is weeks of careful, dull reading.

You do not need a model that writes for this. You need one that **points**.

## What named entity recognition is

**Named entity recognition** (NER) is one of the oldest jobs in language
processing: find the names in a text, and say what kind each one is. A person,
a company, a place, a date.

![An invented sentence, "Mara Feld of Larkmoor Homes signed the lease in Leeds on 3 March 2026", with each name boxed and labelled person, company, place and date](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-ner-sentence.webp)

The classic way to build it is to train a model on thousands of sentences
that people marked by hand. It learns those labels, and only those. If you
want *governing law* or *notice period*, you mark a few thousand more
sentences and train again. That is why NER was, for a long time, a fixed set
of four or five labels.

## What zero-shot means

**Zero-shot** turns that around. Zero means zero examples: the label is not
learned in advance, you write it when you ask. A zero-shot extraction model
reads your label as text, the same way it reads the document, compares it
with every stretch of words, and keeps the stretches that match it closely
enough.

![A label, notice_period, with its one-line description, compared with four stretches of a contract clause; "ninety (90) days' prior written notice" scores 0.97 and the others under 0.05 (an illustration)](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-zero-shot.webp)

So *notice period* stops being a training project. It is one line you type:
*"Notice needed to end the contract, e.g. 'ninety (90) days'"*.

## GLiNER2 and GLiNER2.5

GLiNER2 is one of these models. It was published in 2025 with open weights
(Apache 2.0) and has about 205 million parameters, small enough to run on an
ordinary CPU, where chat models have billions.

It is an **encoder**: it reads the text and never writes a word. Everything
it returns is either a piece of your document, with its position, or one of
your own labels. It does several jobs in one pass: names, a classification
and a structured record, all from one schema.

![What comes back from an extraction model: not a written sentence, but a piece of the document with its character positions, and one of your own labels](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-what-comes-back.webp)

Why so many teams use it:

- **Cost.** Nothing is paid per page.
- **Speed.** It scores all your labels together, so fifty labels take barely
  longer than five (its paper measured 130 ms for 5 labels and 208 ms for 50,
  on a CPU).
- **Privacy.** Contracts, medical notes and personnel files never leave the
  building.
- **Good enough.** On the paper's zero-shot test of finding names in five
  fields, it scored 59.0 F1, within a point of a large chat model's 59.9.

It is used to redact personal data, pull fields out of documents, route
requests and build knowledge graphs.

**GLiNER2.5** came in August 2026. The older model tried every short stretch
of words as a possible answer. The new one predicts where an answer starts
and where it ends, so an answer can be any length and it reads about 4,000
words at a time. It comes in three sizes, from 74 to 287 million parameters;
the largest reads many languages.

![The same words read two ways: every short stretch tried as a candidate, and an answer marked by where it starts and where it ends](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-start-and-end.webp)

## A rule worth keeping

What an extraction model does not do is think. It does not judge, and it does
not calculate.

![Four cards: Point, when it is written in the text; Decide, a judgement with fixed options; Write, when it has to be worked out; Pattern, when it has a fixed shape](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-rule-of-thumb.webp)

- **Written in the text:** a model that points (extraction). Parties, dates,
  notice periods, laws.
- **A judgement with fixed options:** a model that decides. *Does this
  contract renew by itself?* We wrote about these in
  [One Look](/blog/articles/decision-models-system-one-explained/).
- **Something to work out:** a model that writes. The date a notice is due.
- **A fixed shape:** a pattern. An order or account number.

## The data room

To see how far pointing goes, we built a data room that anyone can open.

![The test set: 510 commercial contracts, filed with the SEC by US-listed companies, with lawyers' answers for 41 kinds of clause](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-cuad.webp)

The contracts come from CUAD, a public set made for exactly this test: 510
commercial agreements that US-listed companies filed with the SEC, with
lawyers' answers for 41 kinds of clause. A source reads 200 of them, the
plain-text versions, straight from the public copy on Hugging Face. No
account, no token, no copy in between.

The workspace has one source, one detector, three glossary schemes, three
watches and two cases, all built with the ordinary product.

![The Contract Data Room workspace: one source, SEC contract filings (CUAD), read from Hugging Face, completed in 75 minutes](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-source.webp)

**One detector, one pass.** It names seven facts and asks one question. Each
fact is a short label and a one-line description:

| Fact | Description the model reads |
| --- | --- |
| `party` | A company or person that signs this agreement |
| `agreement_date` | The date the agreement is made or entered into |
| `effective_date` | The date the agreement takes effect |
| `term` | How long the agreement runs or when it expires |
| `renewal` | Wording that renews the agreement |
| `notice_period` | Notice needed to terminate or to stop a renewal |
| `governing_law` | State or country whose laws govern the agreement |

The question is *what kind of agreement is this*: licence, distribution,
services, supply, marketing, partnership or IP transfer. The detector runs
the newer GLiNER2.5, base size, inside the scan, on the scan's own CPU.

![The detector in the editor: each fact a label with a one-line description and an example](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-detector.webp)

**A glossary.** Three schemes say what the outputs mean: a taxonomy of
agreement types, the contract terms, and a list of jurisdictions with US
states under *United States* and everything else under *Outside the United
States*. The governing-law values are looked up in it, so *"the laws of the
State of New York"*, *"New York law"* and *"State of New York"* all become
**New York**.

## Where the laws pile up

Across all two hundred, the laws pile up where you would expect: Delaware in
75 contracts, New York in 49, California in 34, then Nevada, Florida and
Texas.

But **51 contracts name a law outside the United States**: England and Wales
in 16, China in 13, Israel in 7, then Hong Kong, Switzerland, Japan, the
Netherlands, Germany and Singapore. 26 of them name no US law at all.

![Contracts per law as the glossary files them: Delaware 75, New York 49, California 34; outside the United States, England and Wales 16, China 13, Israel 7, Hong Kong 5](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-laws.webp)

The agreement types spread almost evenly: services 39, supply 38, licences 36,
marketing 17, partnerships 13, distribution 8 and IP transfers 2. The
glossary's map puts every one of these concepts, and every jurisdiction, on
one page:

![The meaning map: the contract terms, the agreement types and every jurisdiction found, with US states under United States and the rest under Outside the United States](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-meaning-map.webp)

**Who signs more than once.** 26 companies are named as a party in two or
more contracts: Cardlytics in four, A.M. Best and American International
Group in three. Each became an entity, so every contract a company signed is
one click away from it.

![The entities: companies that sign two or more of the contracts, each with its mentions and the contracts it appears in](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-entities.webp)

## England, three times

Look closer at England, though.

One executive's service agreement grants paid holiday *"in addition to the
usual public or bank holidays in England"*. The model filed **England** as the
governing law, 99% sure.

![A holiday clause: "the usual public or bank holidays in England", with England filed as governing law at 99%](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-holiday-clause.webp)

A second contract names England and Wales only as where a company is
incorporated: *"a company incorporated under the laws of England and Wales"*.
99% again.

![An incorporation clause, "a company incorporated under the laws of England and Wales", filed as governing law at 99%](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-incorporated.webp)

And the real governing-law clause, in the services agreement above, scored
**65%**, the lowest of six values in its row. First came *FCA*, a regulator,
at 100%.

![The governing-law row of one contract: FCA, a regulator, at 100%, and the real clause, "the laws of England and Wales", at 65%](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-real-clause.webp)

The model sees the words *"the laws of England"*. It cannot tell which clause
they sit in. A high score means the words look like a governing law, not that
the clause is one. Across the workspace, 88 of the 1,064 governing-law values,
in 48 contracts, come straight after *incorporated*, *organized* or *formed*
under the laws of: the law a company was set up under, not the law of the
contract.

## How close it came to the lawyers

CUAD comes with the answers lawyers wrote for every contract. A fact counts
as found when one of the extracted values contains the lawyers' answer, or
the other way round; dates are compared as dates.

| Question | Found | Out of | Share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who signed it (at least one party) | 190 | 193 | 98% |
| Which law governs it | 155 | 164 | 95% |
| When it was signed | 124 | 158 | 78% |
| What kind of agreement (where its title says) | 98 | 144 | 68% |
| Does it renew, and how | 4 | 65 | 6% |

*Out of* is the number of contracts for which the lawyers wrote an answer.

![Against the lawyers' answers: 190 of 193, 155 of 164, 124 of 158, and renewal 4 of 65; and a change-of-control question answered yes at 0.82 to 0.99 where the lawyers found none](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-scores.webp)

Renewal clauses are written in too many ways (*"shall continue from year to
year"*, *"unless either party gives notice"*) for one short description.
And asked whether a contract has a change-of-control clause, the model said
yes to nearly all of them: it scored 0.82 to 0.99 on contracts the lawyers
marked as having none. That is a judgement, not a pointer's job.

## The watch and the two cases

That is why the findings go to people, through watches and cases.

**A watch follows a meaning, not a spelling.** *Governed by law outside the
United States* catches every contract the glossary files there, new ones
included, whatever words the contract used.

![The watch "Governed by law outside the United States", which drives the case of the same name](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-watch-drives-case.webp)

**Case one: contracts governed by law outside the United States.** The watch
feeds a case for deal counsel: 26 contracts that name only a foreign law,
each needing a lawyer who knows that law before anyone relies on a reading
of it. The working conclusion: brief local counsel per jurisdiction, starting
with the laws that turn up more than once. A new contract under English or
Chinese law joins the case by itself.

![The case file: 26 of 200 contracts name only a non-US governing law; priority medium, assignee deal counsel, in progress](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-foreign-law-case.webp)

**Case two: notice periods.** 145 contracts state a notice period, from ten
days to twenty-four months. The case tested one idea on those values: *most
contracts need two months' notice or more*. 100 of the 145 do, the middle
value is 90 days, so the idea stands. The model hands over *"twenty-four
months"*; counting back to a date in the calendar is the case's work.

![The case board: the hypothesis "Most contracts need two months' notice or more", supported, 100 of 145](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-notice-hypothesis.webp)

The cases are where the England catch matters. A contract filed under
*Outside the United States* because of a holiday clause is one a lawyer
opens, reads and drops from the case. The model highlights. The glossary says
what it means. People decide.

## What made the difference

**One line of description per fact, with an example.** The description is
the model's only instruction. *"State or country whose laws govern the
agreement, e.g. 'the State of New York'"* works. A longer description that
also listed what the value is *not* made the results worse.

**The right model for the language.** The multilingual version was twice as
slow on these English contracts and returned many more wrong governing-law
values. Pick it for German or French text, not by default.

**Ask the type of a document from its opening.** Asked over the whole
contract, the label that peaked in any one section won: a maintenance
agreement with a long intellectual-property section became an *IP transfer*.
On 23 contracts the agreement type matched the contract's own title 7 times
when judged over the whole text, and 15 times when judged from the opening.

**Rules behind the model.** A validation rule says what a value has to look
like: a governing law has no digits and is not an *Act*; a term mentions
years, months or days; a date contains a digit. On the first full scan these
rules removed 485 of 1,485 governing-law values and half of the term values,
and not one value that matched the lawyers' answers. They do not catch
*England* in a holiday clause: that looks exactly like a governing law.

## Where it falls short

- **It does not judge.** *Is there a clause that does X?* is a question for a
  decision model or a fine-tuned one.
- **It does not know the clause.** It finds *"the laws of England"* wherever
  they are, in a governing-law clause, an incorporation clause or a holiday
  clause.
- **It does not calculate.** It returns *"ninety (90) days prior written
  notice"*, not the date the notice is due.
- **It names roles as parties.** *Licensor*, *the Company* and *Provider* come
  back along with the real company names.
- **It costs CPU time.** About 8 seconds per 1,000 words for each worker on
  the scan's CPU in our setup. Two hundred contracts took 75 minutes. Nothing
  is paid per page, and nothing leaves the building.

## When to use which

- **The answer is written in the text and you want it word for word:** an
  extraction model. Parties, dates, amounts, notice periods, product names,
  personal data without a fixed format.
- **The answer is a judgement with fixed options:** a decision model. Is this
  urgent, which team, does this contract have a change-of-control clause.
- **The answer has to be worked out:** a chat model. Count the days to the
  deadline, summarise the clause.
- **The value has a fixed shape:** a pattern. IBANs, order numbers, card
  numbers.
