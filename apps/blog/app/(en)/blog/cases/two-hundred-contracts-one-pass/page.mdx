A company is about to be sold. The buyer's lawyers open the data room and
find 200 contracts. They ask every one of them the same questions:

- Who signed it?
- When, and until when?
- Does it renew by itself, and how much notice does it take to stop it?
- Which law governs it?
- Does anything change if the company changes hands?

Each answer is written somewhere in the contract. *"This Agreement shall be
governed by the laws of the State of Delaware"* sits on page fourteen. Finding
it two hundred times is weeks of careful, dull reading.

You do not need a model that thinks for this. You need one that **points**.

## Two kinds of model that answer in one pass

Most people meet AI as a chat model. It reads your question and **writes** an
answer, word by word. That is flexible, slow, and paid per word, and it can
write something that is not in the document.

For questions with a fixed shape there is a faster kind of model that does not
write at all. There are two families, and they are easy to confuse.

**A decision model**, also called a **System One** model, answers a question
about the text. You ask *"Does this contract renew automatically?"* and it
returns *yes, 93% sure*. It weighs the whole text and gives you a verdict. We
wrote about them in
[One Look](/blog/articles/decision-models-system-one-explained/).

**An extraction model** shows you the words. You ask for *"renewal: wording
that renews the agreement"* and it returns *"shall automatically renew for
successive one-year terms"*, together with where that sentence is. It can also
sort the text into labels you give it, in the same pass. It never writes a
word of its own: everything it returns is either a piece of the document or
one of your labels.

| | Decision model (System One) | Extraction model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You ask | A question with fixed answers | For the pieces of text that match a description |
| You get back | A verdict and a probability | The exact words, their position, a confidence |
| Can it return something not in the text? | It judges, so yes: a verdict | No |
| Where it runs | Usually at a provider, with an API key | On your own CPU |
| Good at | Judgements: is this urgent, does this clause exist | Facts: names, dates, amounts, notice periods, laws |

A simple way to think about it: the decision model is a colleague who says
*"yes, it renews, I'm fairly sure"*. The extraction model is a colleague with a
highlighter, who hands the page back with the sentence marked.

They are not rivals. Highlight first, judge second.

## The data room, as a workspace

![The Contract facts detector in the editor: the agreement-type question with a one-line description for each label, licence, distribution, services, supply and marketing](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-detector.webp)

The workspace has five parts, all built with the ordinary product.

**The contracts.** A source reads the plain-text versions straight from the
public dataset on Hugging Face. No account, no token, no copy in between.

**One detector, one pass.** The extraction detector names seven facts and one
question. Each fact is a short label and a one-line description:

| Fact | Description the model reads |
| --- | --- |
| `party` | A company or person that signs this agreement |
| `agreement_date` | The date the agreement is made or entered into |
| `effective_date` | The date the agreement takes effect |
| `term` | How long the agreement runs or when it expires |
| `renewal` | Wording that renews the agreement |
| `notice_period` | Notice needed to terminate or to stop a renewal |
| `governing_law` | State or country whose laws govern the agreement |

The question is *what kind of agreement is this*: licence, distribution,
services, supply, marketing, partnership or IP transfer, each with a one-line
description too.

The model is small enough for a laptop, so it runs inside the scan, on the
scan's CPU. Nothing leaves the machine.

**A glossary.** The outputs mean something: a taxonomy of agreement types, the
contract terms, and a list of jurisdictions.
Each detector output is bound to its concept. The governing-law values are
looked up in the jurisdiction list, so *"the laws of the State of New York"*,
*"New York law"* and *"State of New York"* all become **New York**, and New York
sits under **United States**.

**Watches.** Three standing questions follow the meaning, not the words:
stated notice periods; contracts under law outside the United States;
contracts under Delaware law.

**Cases.** Two cases act on them: the contracts that need foreign counsel, and
the notice deadlines to put in a calendar.

## What one pass returns

Take one contract: a manufacturing outsourcing agreement between Nice Systems
Ltd. and Flextronics (Israel) Ltd., filed in 2003, about 20 000 words long. One
pass of the model returns:

| Fact | What it found | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parties | *Nice Systems Ltd.*, *Flextronics Israel Ltd.* | 99%, 96% |
| Agreement date | *January 21st, 2002* | 100% |
| Effective date | *July 1, 2002* | 100% |
| Term | *3 years*, *7 years* | 99% |
| Renewal | *automatic renewal* | 95% |
| Notice | *six (6) months*, *forty-five (45) Days* and others | 100%, 97% |
| Governing law | *state of Israel* | 99% |
| Agreement type | services | 99% |

Every value is a piece of the contract, with its position. Open the governing
law and you see the sentence it came from, and the five other contracts in the
data room that name the same law:

![The governing-law finding in the workspace: the sentence "This Agreement shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the state of Israel", with "state of Israel" highlighted, and below it five other contracts where the same value appears](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-clause-in-context.webp)

The glossary then says what the value means. *state of Israel* is filed under
**Israel**, which sits under **Outside the United States**, so this contract
turns up in the watch for foreign law without anyone writing a rule for Israel:

![The meaning of the value: Governing law, bound to the contract terms; Israel, bound through the jurisdictions lookup; and Outside the United States, implied by Israel](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-meaning-of-a-value.webp)

The same pass also returned things that are not what we asked for, with lower
confidence: *supplier*, *it* and *person* as parties (62 to 74%), *tax* and
*mutatis mutandis* as governing law (90%). The threshold and the rules remove
most of this kind of noise; some always gets through, and the confidence next
to each value is how you tell.

![Everything the one pass extracted from this contract, label by label, with a confidence for each value](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-one-pass.webp)

## How close it came to the lawyers

CUAD comes with the answers lawyers wrote for every contract. We compared the
detector's findings with them. A fact counts as found when one of the
extracted values contains the lawyers' answer, or the other way round; dates
are compared as dates.

| Question | Found | Out of | Share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who signed it (at least one party) | 190 | 193 | 98% |
| Which law governs it | 155 | 164 | 95% |
| When it was signed | 124 | 158 | 78% |
| What kind of agreement (where its title says) | 98 | 144 | 68% |
| Does it renew, and how | 4 | 65 | 6% |

*Out of* is the number of contracts for which the lawyers wrote an answer.
The model gave an agreement type for 153 of the 200 contracts and left the
rest blank rather than guess below the threshold.

The first three are the facts a contract states plainly, in a predictable
place, and the model finds them almost every time. The last row is the honest
one: renewal clauses are written in too many different ways (*"shall continue
from year to year"*, *"unless either party gives notice"*), and a short
description does not catch them. Fine-tuning on a few dozen examples is the way
to raise it; the detector can do that on the same CPU.

## What it found

The 200 contracts hold 1.66 million words. Read on the scan's own CPU, three
contracts at a time, they took 75 minutes and produced 6 482 facts, every one
of them with a meaning in the glossary.

**What kind of agreements.** Services 39, supply 38, licences 36, marketing 17,
partnerships 13, distribution 8, IP transfers 2.

**Which law.** 172 contracts name a governing law the glossary knows. Delaware
leads with 74, then New York 48 and California 34. 50 contracts name a law
outside the United States: England and Wales 15, China 13, Israel 7 and others.
26 of them name **only** a non-US law.

![The meaning map: the contract terms, the agreement types and every jurisdiction found, with US states under United States and the rest under Outside the United States](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-meaning-map.webp)

**Who signs more than once.** 26 companies are named as a party in two or more
contracts: Cardlytics in four, A.M. Best and American International Group in
three. Each became an entity, so every contract a company signed is one click
away from it.

![The entities: companies that sign two or more of the contracts, each with its mentions and the contracts it appears in](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-entities.webp)

**Two cases.** The first collects the 26 contracts under non-US law only:
those need counsel who knows that law before anyone relies on a reading of
them. A watch keeps it current, so a new contract under English or Chinese law
joins the case by itself.

![The watch "Governed by law outside the United States", driving the case of the same name, with its matches](/blog/assets/images/contract-data-room-watch-foreign-law.webp)

The second is about deadlines. 145 contracts state a notice period. The case
tested one idea on the extracted values: *most contracts need two months'
notice or more*. They do. In 100 of the 145 the longest stated notice is 60
days or more; the middle value is 90 days, the longest 24 months. Each of those
is a date that someone has to count back from and put in a calendar, and
counting is the part the model leaves to you.

## What made the difference

Four changes moved the results more than anything else. None of them is
clever; all of them are things you can do in an afternoon.

**One line of description per fact, with an example.** The description is the
model's only instruction. *"State or country whose laws govern the agreement,
e.g. 'the State of New York'"* works. A longer description that also listed
what the value is *not* made the results worse.

**The right model for the language.** The multilingual version of the model
was twice as slow on these English contracts and returned many more wrong
*governing law* values, section numbers and statute names among them. Pick it
for German or French text, not by default.

**Ask the type of a document from its opening.** A contract says what it is in
its title and first paragraphs. Asked over the whole contract, the label that
peaked in any one section won: a maintenance agreement with a long
intellectual-property section became an *IP transfer*. On 23 contracts the
agreement type matched the contract's own title 7 times when judged over the
whole text, and 15 times when judged from the opening.

**Rules behind the model.** A model that points will sometimes point at the
wrong kind of thing: a statute (*"21 C.F.R. Part 50"*) as the governing law, a
defined term (*"Servicing Period"*) as the term. A validation rule says what a
value has to look like: a governing law contains no digits and is not an *Act*;
a term mentions years, months or days; a date contains a digit. On the first
full scan these rules removed a third of the governing-law values and half of
the term values, and not one value that matched the lawyers' answers.

## Where it falls short

**It does not judge.** We also asked whether each contract has a
change-of-control clause, an exclusivity clause, a non-compete or an automatic
renewal, first as yes/no labels and then as wording to point at. As labels it
said yes to nearly everything: change of control scored between 0.82 and 0.99
on contracts the lawyers marked as having none. As wording, it was right on
about a third to a half of the contracts. *Is there a clause that does X?* is
a judgement, and that is a decision model's job, or a fine-tuned model's.

**It does not calculate.** It returns *"ninety (90) days prior written
notice"*. It does not tell you that the notice is due on 2 October. The date is
a calculation: yours, a rule's, or a chat model's.

**It names roles as parties.** Contracts call their parties *Licensor*,
*the Company*, *Provider*. Those are names inside the contract, and the model
returns them along with the real company names.

**It costs CPU time.** About 8 seconds per 1 000 words for each worker on the
scan's CPU in our setup, a few seconds on a laptop. Two hundred contracts took
75 minutes, not one. Nothing is paid per page, and nothing leaves the building.

## When to use which

- **The answer is written in the text and you want it word for word:** an
  extraction model. Parties, dates, amounts, notice periods, product names,
  personal data without a fixed format.
- **The answer is a judgement with fixed options:** a decision model. Is this
  urgent, which team, does this contract have a change-of-control clause.
- **The answer has to be worked out:** a chat model. Count the days to the
  deadline, summarise the clause, explain the risk.
- **The value has a fixed shape:** a pattern. IBANs, order numbers, card
  numbers.
