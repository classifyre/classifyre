Monday. A pipe bursts, a bedroom floods, and by evening the job has left its
trail on the shared drive: a report, a voice memo, photographs, a scanned
delivery note.

Three weeks later somebody asks one thing: show me everything about the
bedroom ceiling. A search for words finds the report, because the report has
the words. The photograph of the ceiling has no words in it at all.

![A black table with a hard drive, an inspection report stamped FOUND, a tape recorder, a scanned delivery note and a photograph of a stained ceiling with a tag: no words in it](/blog/assets/images/photo-with-no-words-search-misses.webp)

This post is about the thing that closes that gap: vectors. What they are,
how search by meaning works on an ordinary processor, where it stops, and how
a model learns to look at a picture and to listen to a recording.

## What a vector is

A vector is a list of numbers that stands for what something means. A model
reads a sentence and writes those numbers down.

Take just two numbers, and every sentence is a point on a map. Sentences that
mean the same land close together, even when they share no words: "a burst
pipe" and the German *Rohrbruch* get nearly the same two numbers, "holiday
rota" very different ones.

![Three sentences on paper tabs, each with two numbers: a burst pipe −0.79, 0.61; Rohrbruch −0.80, 0.60; holiday rota 0.40, 0.92](/blog/assets/images/vector-two-numbers-alike.webp)

To search, you put the question on the same map and look at what lies
nearest. Near is measured as the angle between two arrows from the middle:
the smaller the angle, the closer the meaning. That is cosine similarity,
and it is what Classifyre compares.

![A map on squared paper with arrows from its middle: a small lime angle between the question and "water through the ceiling", a wide dashed one to "holiday rota"](/blog/assets/images/vector-map-angle-between-arrows.webp)

Real models use hundreds or thousands of numbers rather than two, but the
idea is the same.

## Text search on the processor

The simplest setup runs on any computer, and it is what Classifyre does out
of the box:

1. Every file is read as text: Word files, sheets and mail as they are,
   pictures through a reader that looks for letters (OCR), recordings through
   a transcriber.
2. The text is cut into passages of up to 1,200 characters, each overlapping
   the last by 200.
3. A small built-in model turns each passage into 384 numbers, on the
   processor of the machine Classifyre runs on. Nothing leaves it.

It is quick. On this drive the model wrote down 716 pieces of text in 12
seconds, and a question took 3 to 4
thousandths of a second.

![Three passages of a report going into a chip labelled a small model, coming out as rows of numbers; 716 pieces of text in 12 seconds, one question in 3 to 4 ms](/blog/assets/images/text-search-passages-384-numbers.webp)

Ask "water coming through the bedroom ceiling" and the voice memo recorded on
site and the inspection report come first: both have the words. For text, this
is a good place to start.

## Where it stops

### The photograph

A picture only becomes text through a reader that looks for letters, and a
stain on a ceiling has none. Nothing goes on the map, so no question ever
brings it up. On this drive three of the five photographs have no writing in
them: the ceiling, the mould in the corner behind the wardrobe, the pipe
under the sink.

![The ceiling photograph, a magnifying glass labelled a reader looks for letters, an empty sheet labelled no letters, and a small map with the report, the memo and the delivery note but no photograph, stamped NOT ON THE MAP](/blog/assets/images/gap-photo-not-on-the-map.webp)

### The language

The small model learned English. Asked in German, "Schimmel im Schlafzimmer
hinter dem Schrank" (mould in the bedroom behind the wardrobe), it put the
German memo first. In second place was a sentence from the drive's sample
texts about two people asleep on a sofa: *Ein Mann und eine Frau schlafen auf
dem Sofa.* It shares letters with *Schlafzimmer*, not its meaning. The English
inspection report, which describes exactly that mould, was not in the first
twelve.

![A search slip with the German question and the results: 1. the German memo, 2. d-030.txt about a sofa, marked; below, the English report on the same mould, not in the first 12, stamped MISSED](/blog/assets/images/gap-german-question-sofa-sentence.webp)

## How a model learns to look

A model that reads pictures closes the first gap. It cuts a picture into
small squares, reads them the way a text model reads words, and writes the
same kind of list of numbers, on the same map as the text. A photograph of a
stained ceiling lands next to the sentence "water came through the ceiling".

![The ceiling photograph with a lime grid over it, rows of squares labelled read like words, a short vector, and a map where the photograph lands next to "water through the ceiling"](/blog/assets/images/picture-cut-into-squares-same-map.webp)

It learns that from pictures paired with their captions: each picture is
pulled towards its own caption and pushed away from all the others.

![Three photographs joined by lime threads to their own captions, with dashed lines to the others: a stained, sagging ceiling; black mould in a corner; a leaking pipe under a sink](/blog/assets/images/pictures-pulled-to-their-captions.webp)

A larger model like this also closes the second gap. It learned many
languages, so a German question lands next to an English answer. Asked the
German mould question by meaning alone, the first two texts it returned were
English files about the same wardrobe: the readings ("wall behind wardrobe
27.4") and the walkthrough video ("this is the corner behind the wardrobe,
where the mould is"). The sofa sentence was not in the first forty.

## Sound, when the words are not the point

Sound works the same way. A recording is turned into a picture of its sound,
cut into slices a fraction of a second long, and each slice is read like a
word. In Classifyre a recording is embedded in clips of ten seconds, so that a
sound under somebody's voice gets a clip, and a vector, nearly to itself.

That matters when the words are not the point. At two in the morning a
neighbour leaves a message on the office phone: *Hello. It's flat one at 14
Alder Row. Sorry it's so late. It's started again. Just listen.* Then fifteen
seconds of water running in her hallway, and *Somebody come round in the
morning, please.*

A transcript has her words and nothing else. Asked "water running", text search
did not return her message in the first twelve. The water is only in the
sound.

![Her voicemail as a waveform with the transcript under the speech, "no words" under the fifteen seconds of water, marked in lime; below, a text-only search for "water running" with her message not in the first 12, stamped MISSED](/blog/assets/images/voicemail-water-only-in-the-sound.webp)

There was a second voicemail, from the building's caretaker: *Can you hear
that?* over a smoke alarm. That one did not stand out. To the model, an alarm
going off in a stairwell sounds close to a baby crying and a rooster, and both
were in the drive's sample sounds, so his message came well down the list.
Distinct sounds are found; sounds that resemble others in the same corpus are
ranked among them.

## In Classifyre

This is a setting, not a project. Under **Harness AI → Embedding**:

1. **Model.** Out of the box it is *Local inference*: the built-in model on
   the processor. For pictures and sound, choose *Remote provider* and pick a
   saved AI provider whose model takes them. The card under it says what the
   model also takes (pictures, sound).
2. **What is embedded.** Text is always embedded. Turn **Pictures** on, and
   **Sound** if you want recordings embedded as sound as well.
3. **Scan.** The next scan of a source reads each picture and recording once
   more, and they land on the map next to the text.

Changing the model embeds everything again from scratch, and moving back to a
model that reads only text deletes what was stored for pictures and sound. The full
reference is in the docs:
[Settings → Embeddings](https://docs.classifyre.com/settings/embeddings/#embeddings).

![The embedding settings of the workspace with Pictures and Sound switched on and the line "the next scan reads them again" marked](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-embedding-pictures-switch.webp)

After the scan this drive held 802 vectors, 77 of them pictures and sound
clips. The same questions, before and after:

| black mould in the corner of a room | the inspection report | **the corner photograph** |

None of the three photographs has a word in it, and her voicemail has none of

which one it is.

## What a vector will not do

- **It says that two things are alike, not why.** A photograph near your
  question is a lead, not an answer.
- **It is vague about exact things**, such as a job number or a name. Asked
  "HR-2242" by meaning only, the drive returned three unrelated pictures and
  a voicemail before the note for job HR-2242. A name behaved the same way. That
  is why Classifyre's search box ranks by meaning, by the words in the
  passages and by file names together, and merges the three lists.

| "HR-2242" | First result | Where the job's note came |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning only | an unrelated picture | 5th |
| The search box (meaning and words) | the job's note | 1st |

![A search slip with HR-2242 and two result sheets: by meaning only, pictures and a voicemail first and the job's note fifth; in the search box, the job's note first](/blog/assets/images/vector-vague-about-job-number.webp)

- **A remote model sees what it embeds.** With a provider's model, the text
  and, once they are on, the pictures and recordings are sent to that
  provider. The built-in model keeps everything on the machine.

## A rule of thumb

Start with text, on your own processor. Add a model that reads pictures and
sound when your answers live in them. And check the first results with your
own eyes, on questions whose answers you know.

How to set it up:
[docs.classifyre.com/settings/embeddings](https://docs.classifyre.com/settings/embeddings/#embeddings).
