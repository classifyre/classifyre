Two invoices for the same job. Two dehumidifiers, one air mover, nine days.
One for £612.00, one for £598.50, a day apart. Different names at the top:
Wexcombe Drying Hire Ltd, and Wexcomb Dry Hire Ltd. The same bank account at
the bottom.

![Two invoices on a black table, the supplier names and the bank account lines gone over in lime, tagged two names and one bank account, and a stamp: same or not?](/blog/assets/images/two-invoices-one-bank-account.webp)

One supplier billing twice, or two suppliers? Before anybody can ask, something
has to notice that these two belong together. This post is about how a
machine decides that two documents are the same thing, how you tune it, and
what to do when it is right.

## What a machine compares

A machine does not read the two invoices the way you do. Detectors pull out
the values in them: names, dates, phone numbers, bank accounts. Then the two
lists are compared.

A value both documents hold is a thread between them. A value only one of them
holds is a loose end. Values are tidied before they are compared (lower case,
spaces collapsed, a phone number reduced to its digits), so `020 7946 0588`
and `02079460588` are the same value. "Orrin Fairweather" and "Oren
Fairwether" are not: spelled differently, they are different values.

![The values pulled out of both invoices in two columns, lime threads between the phone, the bank account and the nine days, loose ends on the rest](/blog/assets/images/values-threads-and-loose-ends.webp)

## Weights, in plain words

Not every thread is worth the same. Two letters that both say "nine days"
tell you nothing. Two invoices paying into one bank account tell you a lot.
So every kind of value carries a weight. These are the shipped defaults, and
every one can be changed:

| A shared… | weighs |
| --- | --- |
| date, place, organisation, anything not listed | 1 |
| name | 2 |
| address | 3 |
| phone number | 4 |
| email address | 5 |
| bank account (IBAN), card number, passport | 6 |

The score is the share of all that weight, on both sides, that found a
partner. For the two invoices, at the default weights:

| Kind | Weight | Values (one + other) | In play | Matched |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bank account | 6 | 1 + 1 | 12 | 12 |
| Phone | 4 | 1 + 1 | 8 | 8 |
| Dates | 1 | 3 + 3 | 6 | 2 |
| Names | 2 | 2 + 1 | 6 | 0 |
| Companies | 1 | 1 + 2 | 3 | 0 |
| **Total** | | | **35** | **22** |

22 ÷ 35 = **0.63**. The names and the company names count against the score:
they were in play and found no partner.

![The worked example on paper: a balance, the weight ladder, and the table that ends 22 divided by 35 equals 0.63, stamped: how much of the evidence agrees](/blog/assets/images/match-weight-worked-example.webp)

So the score is not how alike two files feel. It is how much of their
evidence agrees. That is also why a short record with two matching values can
outscore a long report that shares twenty values out of four hundred: the
short record really is more likely to be the same thing.

## The second engine: repeated text

Values catch an account number. They miss a paragraph written twice. For
that, a second engine turns text into lists of numbers that place it by
meaning; [our explainer on vectors](/blog/articles/search-by-meaning/) (and
[the film](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=25SHwzepPKE)) shows how.

In duplicate review it has one job. It spots the same passage on many
documents: a letterhead, a footer, a template. That is furniture, not
evidence, and knowing it is furniture is what lets you stop it from driving
matches.

## Two lines

Two cutoffs decide what happens to a score:

| Below the review line | Between the lines | Above the merge line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dropped | Your queue | Treated as the same thing; nobody looks |

The shipped lines are 0.30 and 0.60. The two invoices, at 0.63, land just
above the merge line: filed as the same thing, without a person seeing them.
Where you draw the merge line decides what you never see.

![A ruler from 0 to 1 with the review line at 0.30, the merge line at 0.60, the queue between them in lime, and the two invoices pinned at 0.63: what you never see](/blog/assets/images/review-and-merge-cutoffs.webp)

## Tuning the noise away, one change at a time

The firm's shared drive in Classifyre had 146 matched pairs after the scan,
100 of them above the merge line. Pulling the merge line up to 0.9 for a
moment (dragging it changes only your view until you save) brought them into
the queue. At the top: 38 pairs of the firm's own invoices, to six different
customers.

**1. The firm's own values.** One such pair scored 0.86. Different customers,
different jobs; what they shared was the firm's own bank account, its phone,
its address and its payment terms. Marking it *Not a duplicate* opens **What
made these match**, which lists every shared value with how many documents
hold it: each of these was in eight or nine. The same labels had made 25
other pairs. Ticking the four values and pressing **Stop matching on these
values** writes one rule per value, on its label. The label itself keeps
counting: the firm's own bank account stops matching, a supplier's bank
account on two invoices still does.

![The What made these match dialog: the firm's bank account, phone, payment terms and address, each in 8 or 9 documents, the same combination produced 25 other pairs, and Stop matching on these values](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-what-made-these-match.webp)

**2. The footer.** After the re-score the values were gone, and what was left
was the invoice footer itself. The repeated-text engine filed it as a *rule
candidate*: 25 pairs. Nothing was left to exclude, so the pattern's pairs were
recorded as not duplicates in one action. One engine says what matched; the
other says it was furniture.

![A repeated-text pattern marked rule candidate, 25 pairs across 8 invoices](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-repeated-text-footer.webp)

**3. Weights.** We tried dates at zero. Seven pairs that shared nothing but a
place name jumped to a perfect 1.00. Zero does not mean "ignore a date when it
disagrees". It means the date is not there, and the evidence against goes
with it:

| One shared place name, three dates on each side, none shared | Score |
| --- | --- |
| Dates at 1 | 2 ÷ 8 = 0.25 |
| Dates at 0 | 2 ÷ 2 = 1.00 |

Dates went back to 1. Names went from 2 to 1 instead: a signature puts the
same name on every letter. The pattern of pairs that matched on a name alone
went from 23 pairs at an average of 0.65 to 21 at 0.58.

![The tuning screen with dates set to 0, and a panel working one pair through: 0.25 with dates at 1, 1.00 with dates at 0](/blog/assets/images/dates-at-zero-worked-example.webp)

**4. The lines.** Review at 0.40, merge at 0.85, saved. Nothing under 0.40 is
stored any more, and 73 pairs were left to look at. The two invoices from the
start, now 0.71, sit between the lines: at the shipped 0.60 they would have
been filed as the same thing with nobody looking.

| | Matched pairs | Above merge | To review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| After the scan, shipped lines | 146 | 100 | 46 |
| After the four steps | 141 | 36 | 73 undecided |

## Reading a pair

The supplier pair, after tuning, read from the top:

- **Match weight**, 0.71: the share of the weighted evidence that matched.
- **Green and red**: green values are on both sides, red ones on one side
  only.
- **Why it scored**: one bar per kind of value. The bars add up to the number
  above; nothing is hidden in a blend. The bank account (+0.39) and the phone
  (+0.26) carry it; the names pull it down.
- **Linked documents**: these two are not joined into a cluster (no link here
  reaches the merge line), so the panel shows the documents matched to either
  of them. One is a new-supplier request, matched to both at 0.77 and 0.67. In
  a cluster the same panel shows the cluster and marks the weakest link, the
  one to cut if a chain has joined two things that do not belong together.
- **The values behind this match**: each kind, its weight, what it
  contributed, and the shared values. The search button on a row shows
  **where else the value appears**.

![Why it scored 0.71: the bank account and phone bars carry it, names and companies pull it down; beside it, the new-supplier request linked to both invoices](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-why-it-scored.webp)

Where else does the bank account appear? In four documents. One is the
approved supplier list, where it has belonged to supplier S-007, Wexcombe
Drying Hire Ltd, since 2 November 2022. So the "new" supplier is paid into an
old supplier's account, and both billed for the same machines, the same nine
days, the same job.

![Where else this value appears: the bank account in four documents, the approved supplier list among them](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-where-else-supplier-list.webp)

## Where the match goes

From the pair it goes two ways:

- **Add to case** puts both invoices on a case board with what was found in
  them. The rest of the pair's neighbourhood is offered too: here, the
  new-supplier request. The case held 3 documents and their 23 findings.
- **Create inquiry watch** keeps watching for the values that carried the
  score: that bank account and that phone number, however they are spaced or
  capitalised, in the same source. On the day it had eight matches (both
  values in each of four documents). The next invoice that carries them lands
  there.

![An inquiry watch: whose value is the bank account or the phone number, eight matches](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-inquiry-watches-values.webp)

## Decisions

Every pair ends in a verdict:

| Verdict | Means | Later scans |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirm | The same thing | Unchanged |
| Not a duplicate | Different | Kept apart |
| Split | The pair should not hold a cluster together | Kept apart, cluster rebuilt |
| Unsure | An answer, not a way out | Unchanged |

**Decisions** is where they collect: what you judged, the score you judged it
at, and where it went. **Went nowhere yet** lists duplicates you confirmed and
never took anywhere: here, a revised invoice and the second draft of a letter.
Usually that is fine. When it is not, select them and send them to a case or
an inquiry. And come back after you tune: a verdict keeps the score it was
made on, and a pair re-scored since is marked "Re-scored since you decided.
Worth another look."

![Decisions filtered to went nowhere yet: a revised invoice and a second draft, both confirmed and not used](/blog/assets/images/classifyre-decisions-went-nowhere.webp)

## What this does not do

- **It merges nothing and deletes nothing.** It records judgements; your files
  stay as they are.
- **It cannot tell you why there are two supplier records**: a mistake, a
  rename, or not. That is what the case is for.
- **It weighs what it is told to weigh.** The weights are yours; the
  defaults are a start, not an answer about your data.

## Two habits

Read the shared values before the score. And change one thing at a time,
then look.
